# The Chida

_חיים יוסף דוד אזולאי — החיד״א_

Among the great figures of Sephardic Jewish history, Rabbi Chaim Yosef David Azulai, known as the **Chida (חיד״א)**, holds a special place. A Torah scholar, halachic authority, kabbalist, traveler, and passionate student of Jewish literature, he dedicated his life to learning, teaching, and preserving the wisdom of generations.

His story connects Jerusalem with the great Jewish communities of North Africa and Europe. Through his books, he helped preserve the names of rabbis, the history of Jewish scholarship, and works that might otherwise have been forgotten.

## Born in Jerusalem

The Chida was born in Jerusalem in 1724, into a distinguished Sephardic rabbinic family. His father, Rabbi Isaac Zerachia Azulai, was a learned Torah scholar and his first teacher. The family traced its scholarly heritage to earlier rabbis and kabbalists, including Rabbi Abraham Azulai, known for his work _Chesed LeAvraham_.

Growing up in Jerusalem, the Chida studied Torah in an environment shaped by generations of scholarship. From an early age, he displayed an extraordinary memory, a deep curiosity, and a remarkable ability to retain and organize what he learned.

His interests extended well beyond a single area of Torah. He studied Talmud and halacha, explored the inner dimensions of Torah, and became fascinated by the authors, books, and historical traditions of the Jewish people.

## The Rabbis Who Shaped His Learning

The Chida learned from several outstanding scholars of his time. Among the most important were:

- **Rabbi Chaim ibn Attar — the Ohr HaChaim (1696–1743):** The celebrated Moroccan-born Torah scholar and kabbalist whose commentary on the Torah, _Or HaChaim_, became one of the most influential works of Jewish biblical interpretation. The Chida studied under him in Jerusalem and held him in profound esteem.

- **Rabbi Yonah Navon:** A leading Jerusalem scholar who was among the Chida’s teachers and helped shape his development in Torah scholarship.

- **Rabbi Isaac HaKohen Rapoport:** A prominent rabbi and halachic authority from the Jerusalem scholarly world.

- **Rabbi Shalom Sharabi — the Rashash (1720–1777):** The renowned Yemenite-born kabbalist who led the Beit El circle of kabbalists in Jerusalem. The Chida was connected with the same world of mystical learning and is often associated with the Rashash in accounts of his education.

These influences helped form the breadth of the Chida’s scholarship. He combined rigorous Talmudic study and practical halacha with an interest in Kabbalah, prayer, biblical interpretation, and the history of Jewish learning.

## A Scholar Who Traveled the World

In the eighteenth century, Jewish communities in the Land of Israel depended in part on support from Jewish communities abroad. Learned emissaries, known as _shadarim_, traveled to raise funds and strengthen connections between communities.

The Chida became one of these emissaries. Beginning in the 1750s, he traveled extensively through Jewish communities in Europe and the Mediterranean. His journeys took him to places including Italy, France, England, Holland, Germany, Egypt, and other parts of the region.

These journeys were more than fundraising missions. Wherever he went, the Chida sought out libraries, examined manuscripts, and investigated the history of Jewish books. He was interested not only in what the great scholars had taught, but also in who they were, which works they had written, and how their teachings had been transmitted.

His travels gave him an unusual perspective on Jewish life across different countries. They also supplied material for one of his most important contributions: documenting the people and literature of the Jewish tradition.

## His Most Important Books

The Chida wrote dozens of works across a wide range of subjects. Several remain especially important.

### _Shem HaGedolim_ — שם הגדולים

Often considered his signature achievement, _Shem HaGedolim_ is a biographical and bibliographical encyclopedia of Jewish scholarship.

The work records the names and information about rabbis, authors, and other Jewish scholars, alongside information about their writings. Its companion section, _Va'ad LaChachamim_, catalogs books and discusses their contents.

At a time when historical records could be scattered across manuscripts and private collections, the Chida worked to gather and preserve this knowledge. His careful attention to names, sources, and authors helped later generations understand the development of Jewish literature.

In this sense, he was not only a Torah scholar but also a preserver of Jewish memory.

### _Birkei Yosef_ — ברכי יוסף

A major work of halacha, _Birkei Yosef_ examines questions of Jewish law and engages with earlier authorities. It reflects the Chida’s broad knowledge of rabbinic sources and his ability to bring different opinions into conversation.

### _Sha'ar Yosef_ — שער יוסף

One of his early works, _Sha'ar Yosef_ is a Talmudic commentary on the tractate Horayot. The Chida began writing at a remarkably young age, demonstrating the scholarly discipline that would characterize his life.

### _Machazik Beracha_ — מחזיק ברכה

Another important halachic work, _Machazik Beracha_ discusses Jewish law and builds on the tradition of earlier authorities. Together with his other legal writings, it demonstrates the practical dimension of his scholarship.

### _Yosef Tehillot_ — יוסף תהילות

A commentary on Tehillim, the Book of Psalms, this work reflects the Chida’s engagement with biblical interpretation and the spiritual life of prayer.

### _Ma'agal Tov_ — מעגל טוב

Unlike his legal and scholarly books, _Ma'agal Tov_ preserves aspects of the Chida’s travels and experiences. It offers a window into the Jewish communities, personalities, and circumstances he encountered, making it valuable to readers interested in both Jewish history and the life of the author himself.

## His Final Years in Livorno

After years of travel and scholarship, the Chida settled in Livorno, Italy, in 1778. Livorno was an important center of Sephardic Jewish life and Hebrew printing, making it a fitting place for a scholar devoted to preserving and publishing Jewish literature.

There he continued writing and bringing his works to print. He died in Livorno in 1806, leaving behind an extraordinary body of scholarship. His remains were later reinterred in Jerusalem.

His legacy continues through the many books he authored and the generations of scholars who have relied on his research.

## A Legacy of Learning and Preservation

What makes the Chida remarkable is the combination of his commitments. He was deeply rooted in Torah, halacha, and Kabbalah, yet he also looked outward: traveling between communities, visiting libraries, studying manuscripts, and recording the contributions of scholars from many places and periods.

His work reminds us that Jewish learning is not only about receiving wisdom. It is also about preserving it, asking where it came from, recognizing the people who carried it forward, and making it available to those who will learn after us.

For a community devoted to Torah study, the Chida’s life offers an enduring example of curiosity joined with dedication: a life spent learning deeply, honoring those who came before, and helping wisdom travel from one generation to the next.

**May the study of his works bring greater understanding, strengthen our connection to the generations before us, and inspire us to continue learning together.**
